## Primary - English P5: Grammar - Week 1: Past and future

### Past simple: regular verbs
File name: `primary-english-p5-en-week1-m1.m4a` (or .mp3, .wav, .ogg)

Past simple: regular verbs.

Think first.

What did you do yesterday after you woke up? Say one thing out loud.

When do we use the past simple?

We use the past simple for things that happened before now and are finished.

These time words tell you a sentence is about the past:

yesterday.

last week, last month, last year.

two days ago.

on Sunday (the Sunday that has gone).

Regular verbs: add -ed.

Most verbs are regular. To make the past simple, add -ed.

play becomes played.

help becomes helped.

walk becomes walked.

clean becomes cleaned.

Examples:

I visited my aunt in Nyamata on Saturday.

We cleaned the classroom yesterday.

She helped her mother in the garden last week.

Three spelling rules.

The verb ends in e: add only -d. Live becomes lived. Dance becomes danced.

The verb ends in a consonant and y: change y to -ied. Study becomes studied. Carry becomes carried.

A short verb ends in one vowel and one consonant: double the last letter. Stop becomes stopped. Plan becomes planned.

Be careful: play becomes played, not plaied, because there is a vowel before the y.

After "did" and "did not".

After did or did not, use the plain verb with no -ed.

She did not play football yesterday.

Did you help your father?

Example.

Put the verb in the past simple: Yesterday I (wash) my uniform.

Wash is a regular verb.

Add -ed: washed.

Yesterday I washed my uniform.

Try it.

Last night we (watch) a football match. Hint: add -ed.

She (study) English last year. Hint: the verb ends in a consonant and y.

Check your answers: watched, studied.

Think back.

Tell a friend two things you did last weekend. Use two verbs that end in -ed.

### Past simple: irregular verbs and time connectors
File name: `primary-english-p5-en-week1-m2.m4a` (or .mp3, .wav, .ogg)

Past simple: irregular verbs and time connectors.

Think first.

Which sounds right: "I goed to school" or "I went to school"?

Irregular verbs.

Some verbs do not take -ed. They change in their own way. We call them irregular verbs. You learn them one by one.

go becomes went.

meet becomes met.

get becomes got.

do becomes did.

eat becomes ate.

see becomes saw.

come becomes came.

have becomes had.

ride becomes rode.

teach becomes taught.

read becomes read (it is spelled the same, but it sounds like "red").

Was and were.

The verb be has two past forms.

I, he, she, it: was. I was seven years old when I started school.

You, we, they: were. They were happy yesterday.

Time connectors.

Time connectors show the order in which things happened. Put a comma after them.

First, for the thing that happened at the start.

Then, for the next thing.

After that, for the thing that followed.

Finally, for the last thing.

Example.

Read this short story. Look at the verbs and the connectors.

Last Saturday I went to the market with my mother. First, we got some beans. Then, we met my teacher. After that, we ate bananas. Finally, we came home.

Every verb is in the past simple. The connectors tell you the order.

Try it.

They (go) to church last Sunday. Hint: go is irregular.

I (be) ten years old when I started Primary 4. Hint: use was or were.

Check your answers: went, was.

Think back.

Tell the story of your morning. Start with "First," and end with "Finally,".

### Future plans: going to, want to, would like to
File name: `primary-english-p5-en-week1-m3.m4a` (or .mp3, .wav, .ogg)

Future plans: going to, want to, would like to.

Think first.

What do you want to be when you grow up?

Talking about the future.

The future is the time that has not come yet. These time words tell you a sentence is about the future:

tomorrow.

next week, next month, next year.

in the future.

when I grow up.

Today you will learn three ways to talk about plans and wishes.

Going to.

Use going to for a plan you have already decided.

Build it like this: am, is or are and then going to and then the plain verb.

I am going to study hard.

She is going to visit her aunt tomorrow.

They are going to plant trees next Saturday.

Do not forget am, is or are. "I going to study" is wrong.

Want to.

Use want to for something you wish for strongly.

I want to build a house for my parents.

We want to win the match.

With he, she or it, add s: He wants to become a teacher.

Would like to.

Use would like to for a wish, in a polite and gentle way. It stays the same for every person.

I would like to be a nurse.

She would like to learn French.

The verb after "to".

After going to, want to and would like to, always use the plain verb.

Correct: I am going to visit my uncle.

Wrong: I am going to visited my uncle.

Example.

Make a sentence: she, going to, cook rice, tonight.

She goes with is.

She is going to cook rice tonight.

Try it.

Check your answers: We are going to clean. He wants to be.

## Primary - English P5: Grammar - Week 2: Comparing things

### Comparing two things: -er than
File name: `primary-english-p5-en-week2-m1.m4a` (or .mp3, .wav, .ogg)

Comparing two things: -er than.

Think first.

Who is taller, you or your best friend?

Comparative adjectives.

An adjective is a describing word: tall, big, cheap, fast.

To compare two people, places or things, we use the comparative. For short adjectives, add -er and then use the word than.

tall becomes taller.

long becomes longer.

deep becomes deeper.

high becomes higher.

cheap becomes cheaper.

fast becomes faster.

Examples:

A bus is bigger than a motorbike.

Lake Kivu is deeper than Lake Muhazi.

My brother is taller than me.

Three spelling rules.

The adjective ends in e: add only -r. Wide becomes wider. Large becomes larger.

A short adjective ends in one vowel and one consonant: double the last letter. Big becomes bigger. Hot becomes hotter. Thin becomes thinner.

The adjective ends in a consonant and y: change y to -ier. Heavy becomes heavier. Easy becomes easier.

Two mistakes to avoid.

Do not forget than. "A bus is bigger a motorbike" is wrong.

Do not use more with -er. "More taller" is wrong. Say taller.

Example.

Compare: a goat, a cow, heavy.

Heavy ends in a consonant and y, so it becomes heavier.

A cow is heavier than a goat.

Try it.

A bicycle is (cheap) than a car. Hint: add -er.

The market is (wide) than the bridge. Hint: wide already ends in e.

Check your answers: cheaper, wider.

Think back.

Compare two things in the room where you are now. Use -er and than.

### More, better, worse
File name: `primary-english-p5-en-week2-m2.m4a` (or .mp3, .wav, .ogg)

More, better, worse.

Think first.

Which costs more: a bus ticket or an aeroplane ticket?

Long adjectives use "more".

Some adjectives are long. They have three or more beats when you say them: ex-pen-sive, beau-ti-ful, com-for-ta-ble, dan-ger-ous.

Long adjectives do not take -er. Put more in front, and use than after.

An aeroplane is more expensive than a bus.

A bus is more comfortable than a motorbike.

This road is more dangerous than that one.

Two mistakes to avoid.

Do not add -er to a long adjective. "Expensiver" is wrong.

Do not use more and -er together. "More cheaper" is wrong. Say cheaper.

Irregular comparatives.

A few adjectives change completely. Learn these five.

good becomes better.

bad becomes worse.

far becomes farther.

many or much becomes more.

little becomes less.

Examples:

A bus is better than a bicycle for a long trip.

A muddy road is worse than a dry road.

Huye is farther from Kigali than Muhanga.

A bus carries more people than a motorbike.

A bicycle needs less money than a car.

Example.

Compare: a taxi, a bus, expensive.

Expensive is a long adjective, so use more.

A taxi is more expensive than a bus.

Try it.

Travelling by boat is (comfortable) than walking. Hint: it is a long adjective.

My marks this term are (good) than last term. Hint: good is irregular.

Check your answers: more comfortable, better.

Think back.

Which is better for you: studying in the morning or in the evening? Say why, using "better than".

### The most of all: -est and "the most"
File name: `primary-english-p5-en-week2-m3.m4a` (or .mp3, .wav, .ogg)

The most of all: -est and "the most".

Think first.

Who is the tallest person in your family?

Superlative adjectives.

The comparative compares two things. The superlative compares one thing with all the others in a group.

For short adjectives, put the in front and add -est.

tall becomes the tallest.

long becomes the longest.

high becomes the highest.

fast becomes the fastest.

The spelling rules are the same as for -er.

big becomes the biggest.

wide becomes the widest.

heavy becomes the heaviest.

Examples:

Mount Karisimbi is the highest mountain in Rwanda.

The Nyabarongo is the longest river in Rwanda.

An aeroplane is the fastest means of transport.

Long adjectives use "the most".

This is the most beautiful dress in the shop.

The taxi is the most expensive way to get to town.

Irregular superlatives.

good, better, the best.

bad, worse, the worst.

far, farther, the farthest.

Two mistakes to avoid.

Do not forget the. Say "the tallest boy", not "tallest boy".

Do not use most and -est together. "The most tallest" is wrong.

Example.

Keza is 1.50 metres tall. Aline is 1.42 metres tall. Jane is 1.38 metres tall.

Keza is taller than Aline. That compares two girls.

Keza is the tallest. That compares her with all of them.

Jane is the shortest.

Try it.

Friday was (hot) day of the week. Hint: double the t and do not forget the.

This is (good) book in the library. Hint: good is irregular.

Check your answers: the hottest, the best.

Think back.

What is the best meal you have ever eaten? Say it in a full sentence.

## Primary - Maths P5: Angles and data - Week 1: Angles and lines

### Angles and cutting them in half
File name: `primary-math-p5-en-week1-m1.m4a` (or .mp3, .wav, .ogg)

Angles and cutting them in half.

Think first.

Open a door a little. Now open it wide. Which opening makes the bigger angle?

What is an angle?

An angle is the opening between two lines that meet at a point. We measure angles in degrees. We write degrees with a small circle, like this: 90°.

A right angle is exactly 90°. The corner of a book is a right angle.

An acute angle is smaller than 90°.

An obtuse angle is bigger than 90° but smaller than 180°.

A straight angle is 180°. It looks like a straight line.

Measuring with a protractor.

First, put the centre of the protractor on the point where the two lines meet.

Then, lay the zero line of the protractor along one line of the angle.

Last, read the number where the other line crosses the scale. Start counting from 0, not from 180.

Bisecting an angle.

To bisect an angle is to cut it into two equal parts.

You can bisect a paper angle by folding it. Fold so that one line lies on top of the other line. The fold cuts the angle in half. You can also bisect with a pair of compasses and a ruler.

Bisecting helps us make new angles:

Bisect 90° and you get 45°.

Bisect 45° and you get 22.5° (twenty-two and a half degrees).

Bisect 60° and you get 30°.

Bisect 30° and you get 15°.

Example.

An angle is 120°. Bisect it.

Two equal parts, so divide by 2.

120 ÷ 2 equals 60.

Each part is 60°.

Try it.

Bisect an angle of 80°. Hint: divide by 2.

An angle was bisected and each part is 35°. How big was the angle at the start? Hint: two equal parts make the whole angle.

Check your answers: 40° and 70°.

### Angles where lines cross
File name: `primary-math-p5-en-week1-m2.m4a` (or .mp3, .wav, .ogg)

Angles where lines cross.

Think first.

Look at the letter X. Two lines cross each other. How many angles can you count?

Perpendicular lines.

Lines that meet at 90° are called perpendicular lines. A wall and the floor are perpendicular. So are the two edges at the corner of your exercise book.

Two angles that add up to 90° are called complementary angles. Example: 30° and 60°.

Angles on a straight line.

Angles that sit next to each other on a straight line add up to 180°. They are called supplementary angles.

If one angle is 130°, the other is 180 minus 130 equals 50°.

Angles at a point.

All the angles that go round one point add up to 360°. That is one full turn.

Opposite angles.

When two straight lines cross, they make four angles. The angles that face each other are called opposite angles. Opposite angles are equal.

Example.

Two lines cross. One angle is 50°. Find the other three.

The opposite angle is equal, so it is 50°.

The angle next to it is on the same straight line: 180 minus 50 equals 130°.

The last angle is opposite the 130°, so it is 130°.

Check: 50 plus 130 plus 50 plus 130 equals 360. Correct.

Try it.

Two angles are on a straight line. One is 110°. Find the other. Hint: take it away from 180.

Three angles meet at a point. Two of them are 100° and 120°. Find the third. Hint: all three add up to 360.

Check your answers: 70° and 140°.

### Parallel lines and triangles
File name: `primary-math-p5-en-week1-m3.m4a` (or .mp3, .wav, .ogg)

Parallel lines and triangles.

Think first.

Look at the two long edges of a ruler. If you made them longer and longer, would they ever meet?

Parallel lines.

Parallel lines stay the same distance apart. They never meet. The two edges of a ruler are parallel. So are the lines in your exercise book.

A line that crosses two parallel lines is called a transversal. It makes angles at both crossings, and these angles follow three rules.

Corresponding angles are equal.

Corresponding angles are in the same position at each crossing. For example, both are above the parallel line and both are on the right of the transversal. They make the shape of the letter F.

Alternate angles are equal.

Alternate angles are between the two parallel lines, on opposite sides of the transversal. They make the shape of the letter Z.

Co-interior angles add up to 180°.

Co-interior angles are between the two parallel lines, on the same side of the transversal. They make the shape of the letter C. They are not equal. They add up to 180°.

Angles in a triangle.

The three angles of any triangle add up to 180°. Cut the three corners off a paper triangle and put them side by side. They make a straight line.

Example.

A triangle has angles of 60° and 70°. Find the third angle.

Add the two you know: 60 plus 70 equals 130.

Take the sum away from 180: 180 minus 130 equals 50.

The third angle is 50°.

Try it.

Two co-interior angles lie between parallel lines. One is 110°. Find the other. Hint: they add up to 180.

A triangle has a right angle and an angle of 35°. Find the third angle. Hint: a right angle is 90°.

Check your answers: 70° and 55°.

## Primary - Maths P5: Angles and data - Week 2: Collecting and showing data

### Kinds of data
File name: `primary-math-p5-en-week2-m1.m4a` (or .mp3, .wav, .ogg)

Kinds of data.

Think first.

Your friend says: "I have 3 goats and they are brown." Which part of that sentence is a number you can count?

What is data?

Data is information that we collect. Data helps us to remember what happened and to plan well. A shopkeeper who writes down what she sells each day is collecting data.

Two big kinds of data.

Quantitative data uses numbers. It answers "how many?" or "how much?".

There are 25 pupils in the class.

The plant is 15 cm tall.

It is 30°C outside.

Qualitative data uses words to describe. It answers "what kind?".

The flower is red.

The soup is salty.

The animal is a dog.

Two kinds of quantitative data.

Discrete data is counted. It uses whole numbers only. There are gaps between the values.

Goals in a match: 4 goals.

Books on a shelf: 12 books.

Cars at the market: 10 cars. You cannot have 10 and a half cars.

Continuous data is measured. It can take any value, with decimals or fractions.

The height of a pupil: 1.40 m.

The mass of a bag of rice: 2.5 kg.

The time to run a race: 12.8 seconds.

A quick test: ask yourself, "Do I count it or do I measure it?".

Example.

Sort these: 7 mangoes, 2.5 kg of rice, the mango is sweet.

7 mangoes: you count them, so it is discrete data.

2.5 kg of rice: you measure it, so it is continuous data.

The mango is sweet: it is a describing word, so it is qualitative data.

Try it.

The number of brothers and sisters you have. Discrete or continuous? Hint: do you count or measure?

The height of a maize plant. Discrete or continuous?

Check your answers: discrete, then continuous.

### Tally charts and bar charts
File name: `primary-math-p5-en-week2-m2.m4a` (or .mp3, .wav, .ogg)

Tally charts and bar charts.

Think first.

You want to know which fruit your classmates like best. How can you keep count while they call out their answers?

Tally marks.

A tally mark is one small stroke for each answer. For every fifth answer, draw the stroke across the four before it. This makes a bundle of 5.

Bundles make counting quick. Count the bundles in fives, then add the strokes that are left.

Two bundles and three strokes: 5 plus 5 plus 3 equals 13.

The total for each group is called the frequency. Frequency means how many times.

Example: favourite fruit.

Twenty pupils chose their favourite fruit. After counting the tally marks, the frequencies are:

Banana: 8.

Mango: 6.

Avocado: 4.

Orange: 2.

Check the total: 8 plus 6 plus 4 plus 2 equals 20. All twenty pupils are counted.

Drawing a bar chart.

A bar chart shows the frequencies as bars.

First, draw two lines: one along the bottom and one up the side.

Then, write the groups along the bottom: banana, mango, avocado, orange.

Then, write numbers up the side. Start at 0 and go up in equal steps.

Draw one bar for each group. The height of the bar is the frequency.

Make every bar the same width, and leave a gap between bars.

Last, give the chart a title and label both lines.

Reading a bar chart.

The tallest bar is the group with the most. Here it is banana.

The shortest bar is the group with the least. Here it is orange.

To compare two bars, subtract. Banana and orange: 8 minus 2 equals 6 more pupils chose banana.

Try it.

Use the fruit data above.

How many pupils chose mango or avocado? Hint: add the two frequencies.

How many more pupils chose mango than orange? Hint: subtract.

Check your answers: 10 and 4.

### Line graphs
File name: `primary-math-p5-en-week2-m3.m4a` (or .mp3, .wav, .ogg)

Line graphs.

Think first.

Is it hotter at 8 in the morning or at midday? How could you show that change on paper?

What is a line graph?

A line graph shows how something changes over time. A bar chart compares groups. A line graph follows one thing as time passes.

Time goes along the bottom: hours, days, weeks or months.

The value you measure goes up the side: centimetres, degrees, francs.

Drawing a line graph.

First, draw the two lines and label them.

Then, write the times along the bottom and the numbers up the side. Start at 0 and go up in equal steps.

### Why we classify, and how to use a key

Why we classify, and how to use a key.

Think first.

In a market, why are tomatoes sold in one place and clothes in another?

So many living things.

Rwanda has thousands of different kinds of plants and hundreds of different kinds of animals. The whole Earth has millions. Nobody can study them one by one without some order.

What is classification?

Classification means sorting living things into groups. We put organisms together when they share features, and we separate them when they are different. The science of classifying organisms is called taxonomy.

You already classify. When you say "that is an animal" and "that is a plant", you are sorting by features: the animal moves about and eats other organisms, and the plant is green and stays in one place.

Why do scientists classify?

It makes organisms easier to identify.

It makes them easier to study, because organisms in one group are alike.

It lets scientists everywhere share what they find.

Scientists sometimes move an organism to a different group when they learn more about it.

Identification keys.

A key helps you to name an organism you do not know. A dichotomous key is a list of steps. Each step gives two statements. You choose the one that fits your organism and follow where it sends you. "Di" means two.

Step 3: It has fins: it is a tilapia. It has no fins: it is an earthworm.

Hierarchical classification.

Living things are sorted in the same way: big groups are divided into smaller and smaller groups. This is called hierarchical classification.

There are seven levels, from the largest to the smallest:

Kingdom.

Phylum.

Class.

Order.

Family.

Genus.

Species.

The same animal has different names in different languages: inka, cow, vache. Scientists in different countries need one name that everyone understands.

### The five kingdoms
File name: `secondary-biology-s1-en-week1-m3.m4a` (or .mp3, .wav, .ogg)

The five kingdoms.

Think first.

A mushroom grows from the ground like a plant. But is it green? Does it make its own food?

Five kingdoms.

All organisms are placed in five big groups called kingdoms: Animalia, Plantae, Fungi, Protoctista and Monera.

Three features are used to decide the kingdom: the structure of the body, the way the organism gets its food, and the way it reproduces.

Animalia (animals).

Made of many cells. The cells have no cell wall.

They feed on other organisms. We say they are heterotrophic.

Most can move from place to place.

Examples: snail, mosquito, snake, bird, human.

Plantae (plants).

Made of many cells. The cells have a cell wall.

They contain a green substance called chlorophyll and make their own food by photosynthesis.

Examples: moss, fern, maize, avocado tree.

Fungi.

They do not photosynthesise. They take food from other living or dead material.

They reproduce by spores, not seeds.

Their cells have a cell wall made of a substance called chitin.

Yeast is a single cell. Moulds and mushrooms have many cells.

Protoctista.

Single cells, or groups of single cells living together.

Each cell has a true nucleus with a membrane around it.

Examples: amoeba, paramecium.

Monera.

Single cells with no true nucleus. There is no membrane around the genetic material.

They are the smallest and simplest organisms.

Example: bacteria.

It is not green and does not photosynthesise, so it is not a plant.

It reproduces by spores.

It belongs to the kingdom Fungi.

## Secondary - Biology S1: Classification and cells - Week 2: Plant and animal cells

### Cells: the building blocks of life
File name: `secondary-biology-s1-en-week2-m1.m4a` (or .mp3, .wav, .ogg)

Cells: the building blocks of life.

Think first.

A house is built from bricks. What do you think a goat or a maize plant is built from?

The cell.

All living things are made of cells. The cell is the basic unit of every living organism. Cells are the building blocks of life, just as bricks are the building blocks of a house.

Cells are made of substances such as water, proteins, carbohydrates and fats.

One cell or many cells.

Some organisms are made of only one cell. They are called unicellular organisms. "Uni" means one. Examples: bacteria, amoeba, yeast.

Other organisms are made of many cells. They are called multicellular organisms. "Multi" means many. Examples: humans, goats, maize plants, trees.

Your own body has a huge number of cells, far too many to count.

Seeing cells.

Most cells are too small to see with the naked eye. Scientists use a microscope to see them.

A light microscope uses light and lenses. It is the kind found in a school laboratory. It shows the shape of cells and their larger parts.

An electron microscope is much more powerful. It shows very small structures that a light microscope cannot show.

From cells to a whole organism.

In multicellular organisms, cells work together in levels.

Cells of the same kind form a tissue.

Different tissues form an organ, such as the heart or a leaf.

Organs work together in the whole organism.

So the order from smallest to largest is: cell, tissue, organ, organism.

Example.

Is a bacterium unicellular or multicellular?

A bacterium is made of only one cell.

So it is unicellular.

### Inside a cell: the parts and their jobs
File name: `secondary-biology-s1-en-week2-m2.m4a` (or .mp3, .wav, .ogg)

Inside a cell: the parts and their jobs.

Think first.

A school has a gate, a head teacher's office and a kitchen. What job does each one do?

Organelles.

A cell is not an empty bag. Inside it are small structures, each with a special job. These structures are called organelles.

Plant cells and animal cells all have the five parts below.

Cell membrane.

The cell membrane is a thin layer around the cell. It keeps the contents of the cell in place.

It is selectively permeable. This means it lets some substances pass and stops others. It controls what goes into the cell and what comes out. It is like the school gate.

Cytoplasm.

The cytoplasm is a living, jelly-like substance that fills the cell. It is mostly water, with substances such as sugars dissolved in it. Many chemical reactions take place there, and the organelles sit in it.

Nucleus.

The nucleus is the control centre of the cell. It is like the head teacher's office.

It holds the hereditary information, also called genetic information, that is passed from parents to their offspring. The nucleus plays an important part when a cell divides to make new cells.

Mitochondria.

Mitochondria are the organelles where cellular respiration takes place. This is where the cell gets energy that it can use. They are like the school kitchen. One of them is called a mitochondrion.

Cells that work hard have many mitochondria. A muscle cell has thousands, because muscles need a lot of energy.

Vacuoles.

A vacuole is a small bag surrounded by a membrane and filled with fluid. It can store water, food or waste products.

Example.

Which part of the cell would you expect to find in large numbers in the leg muscles of a runner?

Running needs a lot of energy.

Energy is released in the mitochondria.

So the muscle cells have many mitochondria.

### Plant cells and animal cells
File name: `secondary-biology-s1-en-week2-m3.m4a` (or .mp3, .wav, .ogg)

Plant cells and animal cells.

Think first.

A goat has bones to hold it up. A tree has no bones. So how does a tree stand tall?

What both kinds of cell share.

Plant cells and animal cells both have a cell membrane, cytoplasm, a nucleus and mitochondria.

Three things only plant cells have.

A cell wall. The cell wall is a rigid layer around the outside of a plant cell, outside the cell membrane. It is made of a substance called cellulose. It is not living. It gives the cell its shape and protects what is inside. It is fully permeable, so substances pass through it freely.

Chloroplasts. Chloroplasts are the organelles where photosynthesis takes place. They contain a green pigment called chlorophyll. This is why leaves are green.

A large central vacuole. A plant cell has one large vacuole that takes up much of the space in the cell. It is filled with a liquid called cell sap: water with mineral salts and food dissolved in it. It helps to keep the cell firm.

Animal cells.

They have no cell wall, so they have many different shapes.

They have no chloroplasts, because animals do not photosynthesise.

They have small vacuoles or none at all.

Where is the nucleus?

In an animal cell the nucleus is usually near the centre. In a plant cell the large vacuole pushes the nucleus to the side.

Under the microscope.

Cells from the skin of an onion look like neat boxes in rows, because each has a cell wall.

Cells from the inside of your cheek look rounded and uneven, because they have no cell wall.

Onion skin cells have no chloroplasts. The onion bulb grows under the soil, where there is no light for photosynthesis.

Example.

A cell has a cell wall, a large vacuole and chloroplasts. What kind of cell is it?

All three are found only in plant cells.

It is a plant cell, probably from a leaf.

## Secondary - Maths S1: Angles and statistics - Week 1: Points, lines and angles

### Lines and types of angles
File name: `secondary-math-s1-en-week1-m1.m4a` (or .mp3, .wav, .ogg)

Lines and types of angles.

Think first.

Look at the edge of your desk. Does it go on forever, or does it have two ends?

Points and lines.

A point marks an exact position. It has no size. We name it with a capital letter, such as A.

A line is straight and goes on forever in both directions.

A line segment is a part of a line with two endpoints. You can measure it. The edge of your desk is a line segment.

A ray has one endpoint and goes on forever in one direction. Think of the light from a torch.

Two special pairs of lines:

Parallel lines are always the same distance apart and never meet.

Perpendicular lines meet at 90°.

Angles.

An angle is formed when two rays or lines meet at a point. The point is called the vertex. We measure angles in degrees.

Types of angles.

An acute angle is less than 90°.

A right angle is exactly 90°.

An obtuse angle is more than 90° and less than 180°.

A straight angle is exactly 180°.

A reflex angle is more than 180° and less than 360°.

A full turn is 360°.

You see these every day. A door open a little makes an acute angle. A door pushed wide open makes an obtuse angle. A flat road is a straight angle.

Example.

135° is more than 90° and less than 180°, so it is obtuse.

200° is more than 180° and less than 360°, so it is reflex.

### Angle facts: straight lines, points and parallel lines
File name: `secondary-math-s1-en-week1-m2.m4a` (or .mp3, .wav, .ogg)

Angle facts: straight lines, points and parallel lines.

Think first.

Two roads cross each other. If you know one of the four angles, can you find the other three without measuring?

Three facts about meeting lines.

Angles on a straight line add up to 180°. Two angles that add up to 180° are called supplementary angles.

Angles at a point add up to 360°.

Vertically opposite angles are equal. These are the angles that face each other when two straight lines cross.

Three facts about parallel lines.

A line that crosses two parallel lines is called a transversal.

Corresponding angles are equal. They are in the same position at each crossing. Look for the letter F.

Alternate angles are equal. They are between the parallel lines, on opposite sides of the transversal. Look for the letter Z.

Co-interior angles add up to 180°. They are between the parallel lines, on the same side of the transversal. Look for the letter C.

Always give a reason.

In geometry you write the fact you used, in brackets, next to each step.

Example one.

Three angles meet at a point: 120°, 95° and a. Find a.

120 plus 95 plus a equals 360 (angles at a point).

215 plus a equals 360.

a equals 360 minus 215 equals 145°.

Example two.

Two angles on a straight line are 2x and x. Find x.

2x plus times equals 180 (angles on a straight line).

3x equals 180.

x equals 60°.

So the two angles are 120° and 60°.

### Angles in polygons
File name: `secondary-math-s1-en-week1-m3.m4a` (or .mp3, .wav, .ogg)

Angles in polygons.

Think first.

A triangle has three angles that add up to 180°. What do you think the four angles of a rectangle add up to?

Polygons.

A polygon is a closed shape with straight sides.

3 sides: triangle.

4 sides: quadrilateral.

5 sides: pentagon.

6 sides: hexagon.

8 sides: octagon.

Sum of the interior angles.

The interior angles are the angles inside the polygon. For a polygon with n sides:

Sum of interior angles equals (n minus 2) × 180°.

Why? From one corner you can cut any polygon into triangles. You always get 2 fewer triangles than sides, and each triangle has 180°.

Triangle: (3 minus 2) × 180 equals 180°.

Quadrilateral: (4 minus 2) × 180 equals 360°.

Pentagon: (5 minus 2) × 180 equals 540°.

Hexagon: (6 minus 2) × 180 equals 720°.

Sum of the exterior angles.

An exterior angle is made when you extend one side of the polygon. The exterior angles of any polygon add up to 360°.

Regular polygons.

A regular polygon has all sides equal and all angles equal.

Each interior angle equals sum of interior angles ÷ n.

Each exterior angle equals 360° ÷ n.

For a regular hexagon: each interior angle is 720 ÷ 6 equals 120°, and each exterior angle is 360 ÷ 6 equals 60°. Notice that 120 plus 60 equals 180, because the two angles sit on a straight line.

Example.

A quadrilateral has angles of 90°, 85° and 110°. Find the fourth angle.

The sum must be 360°.

90 plus 85 plus 110 equals 285.

360 minus 285 equals 75°.

Try it.

Find the sum of the interior angles of an octagon. Hint: n is 8.

Find each interior angle of a regular pentagon. Hint: find the sum first, then divide by 5.

Check your answers: 1,080° and 108°.

### Data and frequency tables
File name: `secondary-math-s1-en-week2-m1.m4a` (or .mp3, .wav, .ogg)

Data and frequency tables.

Think first.

A teacher has the marks of 40 students on loose pieces of paper. How can she see quickly which mark is the most common?

What is statistics?

Statistics is about collecting data, organising it, presenting it and explaining what it shows.

Types of data.

Qualitative data describes with words: favourite subject, colour of a shirt, type of music.

Quantitative data uses numbers: marks, height, number of goals.

Quantitative data is of two kinds.

Discrete data is counted in whole numbers: goals in a match, cars at the market.

Continuous data is measured and can take any value: the mass of a bag of rice, the water in a bottle.

Frequency tables.

Raw data is hard to read. A frequency table lists each value and how many times it appears. That number is the frequency. A tally column helps you count: one stroke for each value, with every fifth stroke drawn across the four before it.

Cumulative frequency.

The cumulative frequency is a running total. Add each frequency to all the ones before it. The last cumulative frequency is the total number of values.

Example.

Twenty students took a short test marked out of 5.

Mark 1: frequency 2.

Mark 2: frequency 3.

Mark 3: frequency 6.

Mark 4: frequency 5.

Mark 5: frequency 4.

Total: 2 plus 3 plus 6 plus 5 plus 4 equals 20 students.

Cumulative frequencies: 2, then 2 plus 3 equals 5, then 5 plus 6 equals 11, then 11 plus 5 equals 16, then 16 plus 4 equals 20.

How many students scored 3 or less? Read the cumulative frequency at mark 3: 11 students.

Try it.

Use the test data above.

How many students scored 4 or less? Hint: read the cumulative frequency at mark 4.

How many students scored more than 3? Hint: add the last two frequencies.

Check your answers: 16 and 9.

Think back.

Why is the last cumulative frequency always equal to the total?

### The mean
File name: `secondary-math-s1-en-week2-m2.m4a` (or .mp3, .wav, .ogg)

The mean.

Think first.

Five friends pick 4, 8, 6, 10 and 7 mangoes. They decide to share them equally. How many does each friend get?

What is the mean?

The mean is the average. It is the value each one would have if the total were shared out equally.

Mean equals sum of all the values ÷ number of values.

Example one: raw data.

A shop sold 12, 15, 9, 18 and 21 sodas on five days. Find the mean.

Sum: 12 plus 15 plus 9 plus 18 plus 21 equals 75.

Number of values: 5.

Mean: 75 ÷ 5 equals 15 sodas per day.

The mean from a frequency table.

When values repeat, use the frequency table. Multiply each value by its frequency, add the answers, then divide by the total frequency.

Mean equals sum of (value × frequency) ÷ sum of frequencies.

Example two: frequency table.

Twenty students took a test marked out of 5.

Mark 1, frequency 2: 1 × 2 equals 2.

Mark 2, frequency 3: 2 × 3 equals 6.

Mark 3, frequency 6: 3 × 6 equals 18.

Mark 4, frequency 5: 4 × 5 equals 20.

Mark 5, frequency 4: 5 × 4 equals 20.

Sum of value × frequency: 2 plus 6 plus 18 plus 20 plus 20 equals 66.

Sum of frequencies: 20.

Mean: 66 ÷ 20 equals 3.3 marks.

Two mistakes to avoid.

Do not divide by the number of rows. There are 5 rows here but 20 students. Divide by 20.

Do not just add the marks 1 plus 2 plus 3 plus 4 plus 5. Each mark must be multiplied by its frequency first.

Finding a missing value.

The mean of 4 numbers is 10. Three of them are 8, 12 and 9. Find the fourth.

The total must be 4 × 10 equals 40.

The three known numbers add up to 29.

The fourth is 40 minus 29 equals 11.

Try it.

Find the mean of 4, 8, 6, 10 and 7. Hint: add, then divide by 5.

A table shows the value 2 with frequency 3 and the value 4 with frequency 2. Find the mean. Hint: divide by 5, not by 2.

Check your answers: 7 and 2.8.

### Median and mode
File name: `secondary-math-s1-en-week2-m3.m4a` (or .mp3, .wav, .ogg)

Median and mode.

Think first.

A shop sells shoes in sizes 38, 39, 40 and 41. Which fact helps the shopkeeper more: the average size, or the size most people buy?

The mode.

The mode is the value that appears most often.

A set of data can have one mode.

It can have more than one mode, when two values tie for most often.

It can have no mode, when every value appears once.

The median.

The median is the middle value when the data is arranged in order.

First, arrange the values from smallest to largest. This step is easy to forget.

Then, find the middle.

If the number of values is odd, there is one middle value. That is the median.

If the number of values is even, there are two middle values. The median is the mean of those two: add them and divide by 2.

Example one: odd number of values.

Data: 7, 3, 9, 5, 3.

In order: 3, 3, 5, 7, 9.

There are 5 values, so the third one is the middle.

Median equals 5.

Mode equals 3, because it appears twice.

Example two: even number of values.

Data: 4, 10, 6, 8.

In order: 4, 6, 8, 10.

The two middle values are 6 and 8.

Median equals (6 plus 8) ÷ 2 equals 7.

Which average should you use?

The mean uses every value. One very big or very small value can pull it away from the rest.

The median is not pulled by one extreme value.

The mode tells you the most common value. It is the one a shopkeeper needs when deciding what to stock.

Try it.

Data: 12, 15, 11, 15, 20, 9.

Find the mode.

Find the median. Hint: put the six values in order first, then use the two in the middle.

Check your answers: the mode is 15 and the median is 13.5.
